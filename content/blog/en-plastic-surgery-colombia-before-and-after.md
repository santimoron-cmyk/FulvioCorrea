People searching for **plastic surgery in Colombia before and after** usually want one thing: proof. A photograph can show what changed for one person. It cannot promise what an operation will do for you. The [American Society of Plastic Surgeons](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/articles/can-you-trust-before-and-after-photos) says these pictures are one part of research, not the decision, and that lighting, angle and pose can change how a result looks when the surgery itself has not changed.

How to check a surgeon, a clinic and a written quote is in the guide to [plastic surgery in Colombia](/plastic-surgery-colombia/). This article is only about how to read the pictures.

## What a matching pair actually shows

A useful pair is two photographs of the same person, taken so that the only intended difference is the operation and the time that has passed. The ASPS [photographic guide](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/Documents/Health-Policy/Guidelines/ASPS-Photographic-Guide-Flyer-2020.pdf) is built for that: standard poses, so a before and an after can be compared without distraction or distortion.

When the conditions do not match, you are comparing the photograph, not the surgery. ASPS lists the usual tricks, and they do not require editing software:

- Different lighting. A brighter "after," or a shadow that hides a fold, can look like a surgical change.
- A different angle, distance or crop. Moving the camera, or the chin, changes the nose, the neck and the waist.
- A different pose. Shoulders back, a twist at the hips, or arms held differently will change the silhouette.
- Makeup, hair, clothing or jewelry in one frame and not the other.

In a 2022 paper in *Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery — Global Open*, Danny J. Soares, Haley N. von Haven and Christina H. Yi scored outcome photos on Instagram against photographic standards published in plastic surgery and endorsed by ASPS and specialty boards: position, lighting, expression, background, zoom, makeup and jewelry, more than one view, timing, and a description of what was done. Their point was not that every imperfect photo is fake. It was that a photo you cannot compare is a photo you should not trust as evidence.

## More than one view

One flattering frame is not a result. Ask for the front, the profile and the three-quarter view, taken the same way before and after. A nose that looks refined from the side and unchanged from the front is a different conversation from a nose that reads the same way in all three. The same is true of the abdomen, the breasts and the flanks: a single angle can hide the part you actually care about.

ASPS also says there is no single industry standard for how these photos are presented. Background, clothing and makeup still change what you think you see. Consistency across a surgeon's own gallery matters more than one dramatic pair.

## Ask when the "after" was taken

Swelling is part of healing, and it is not the same at two weeks and at one year. A photograph taken while you are still swollen is a progress picture. It is not the settled result.

The same 2022 review used a three-month minimum for surgical photographs, the interval the American Board of Plastic Surgery expects on photographs submitted for certification. That is a documentation rule for an exam, not a date on which your result is finished, and it is not a promise about any one operation. It is a reason to ask a simpler question: how many months after surgery was this taken, and is swelling still in the picture?

Scars tell a related story. Surgeons quoted by ASPS note that an incision is often still visible for a long time. A frame described as a few weeks after surgery, with no sign of an incision where an incision had to be made, is a reason to ask how the photo was produced. It is not, by itself, proof of a technique.

## Whose patient is in the photograph?

The picture has to be of a patient the surgeon operated on. A photograph from another office, a stock library, a campaign, or a social-media edit is not his result, even if the caption sounds clinical. ASPS advises you to ask where the image came from, how long after surgery it was taken, and whether it is typical of that surgeon's outcomes rather than an outlier.

[ISAPS](https://www.isaps.org/discover/patients-home/considering-your-procedure-abroad/), writing for people who are considering surgery in another country, asks you to plan carefully because safety rules differ by country. A gallery does not replace that. The [ASPS briefing on cosmetic surgery tourism](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/briefing-papers/briefing-paper-cosmetic-surgery-tourism) tells patients to verify the surgeon and the facility, and to agree on follow-up before they travel. Photographs come after those checks, not instead of them.

Bring the questions to the consultation, not only to a website:

- Were both photographs taken in the same room, with the same lighting and the same pose?
- Which views do you have, and can I see the ones that are less flattering?
- How long after surgery was the after taken?
- What was done, in plain language, and what was not done?
- Is this a typical case for you, or one of the best?
- What can a photograph not tell me about scars, sensation or how the result changes if my weight changes?

## Frequently asked questions

**Do before-and-after photos guarantee my result?**
No. ASPS treats them as one part of research. Your anatomy, the operation and how you heal decide your result, and someone else's photograph is not that plan.

**What should match in the before and the after?**
Lighting, camera angle, distance, background and pose. Makeup, clothing and hair should not differ in a way that does the work credited to surgery.

**How long after surgery should the after be?**
Ask for the date. Early photos still show swelling. A peer-reviewed review of outcome photography used three months as a minimum for surgical documentation, which is not the same as the day a result is finished.
