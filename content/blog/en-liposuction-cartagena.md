**Liposuction in Cartagena** attracts patients from Colombia and abroad who want a clear clinical setting, solid travel connections, and a realistic recovery plan. This guide covers how to prepare for a consultation, organize medical travel, and think through aftercare so you arrive with better questions—not a fixed outcome script.

## What liposuction is—and which expectations are realistic

Liposuction removes localized fat deposits in areas such as the abdomen, flanks, back, arms, or thighs. It is not a general weight-loss method and does not replace nutrition and exercise habits. It is used to improve contour in people near a stable weight, with good skin elasticity and clear motivations.

In consultation, the surgeon assesses fat distribution, skin quality, medical history, and lifestyle. Then you discuss whether the procedure is appropriate, whether combining procedures makes sense, or whether waiting is better. A healthy expectation includes minor asymmetries, prolonged swelling, and gradual contour changes as part of the process.

## Why Cartagena is a frequent destination

Cartagena offers an international airport, serviced lodging, and professionals experienced with medical tourism. For travelers, logistics matter as much as technique: flights, nearby lodging, a companion, and a schedule that allows real rest.

Treat the trip as a medical project, not a vacation. Plan free days before and after, avoid intense sightseeing in the early recovery window, and confirm how you will reach follow-up visits. A quiet setting with air conditioning and help getting around supports recovery better than a noisy hotel far from the care team.

## How to prepare for the consultation

Bring or send ahead a brief medical history, allergies, medications and supplements, prior surgeries, and recent tests. Front, profile, and three-quarter photos with even lighting help when the first assessment is virtual.

Ask: Am I a candidate based on weight and skin? Which areas should we prioritize and why? What anesthesia is planned? How many follow-ups and in what format? Which warning signs should I report immediately? How is follow-up coordinated if I live outside Cartagena? Write down the answers.

Also discuss general risks (hematoma, seroma, infection, contour irregularities, temporary changes in sensation) and how they are mitigated. Look for a transparent plan and an open communication channel rather than outcome guarantees.

## Traveling to Cartagena: medical logistics

Book your flight with margin. Ask your surgical team how early you should arrive so you can settle in, complete any missing labs if needed, and rest before surgery—arrival timing varies by case and should not be assumed from a blog. Confirm fasting rules, medications to pause only under medical direction, and comfortable clothing. A companion helps with walking, medication, light meals, and contacting the clinic.

Avoid excessive stairs, heavy luggage, and long trips without breaks in the first days after surgery, as advised by your team. If you are coming from abroad, check documents, travel insurance when applicable, and a local phone or eSIM. Choose lodging with easy clinic access and an elevator if possible, and ask about follow-up and travel-clearance criteria before locking a rigid return ticket: clearance to travel depends on clinical progress.

## Surgery day and the early recovery window

Follow the fasting and hygiene instructions you receive. Wear clothes that are easy to put on and take off, leave jewelry and makeup behind, and arrive with time to spare. Afterward, discomfort, fatigue, and swelling are common; the team will guide you on pain relief, antibiotics if indicated, and whether a compression garment is recommended in your case.

Prioritize relative rest: short walks as directed, hydration, light meals, and sleep. If a garment is prescribed, wear it as instructed—fit, cleaning, and duration are surgeon-specific. For fever, disproportionate pain, increasing redness, heavy drainage, or breathing difficulty, contact the emergency number your team provides right away.

## How recovery usually unfolds (ask your surgeon for your plan)

Swelling can last weeks, and contour continues to evolve. Return to desk work and intense exercise is defined by the surgeon case by case. Do not compare your pace with social media: age, treatment area, technique, and aftercare change the response.

Skin care, sun protection, and any adjunct therapies (for example, lymphatic massage if recommended) should follow written instructions from your care team—who performs them, when they start, and when they stop. If you live elsewhere, ask how virtual follow-ups and photo check-ins will work once you are home, and keep a short log of medication and symptoms if that helps you communicate clearly.

## Pre-op documents and common planning mistakes

Depending on age and history, labs, an electrocardiogram, or an anesthesia evaluation may be requested. Do not stop medications on your own. Disclose herbal supplements that may increase bleeding. International patients should confirm whether studies from their home country are accepted or should be repeated in Cartagena; coordinating this with Sofía, our virtual assistant, before travel avoids delays.

Common mistakes: a return flight too soon, underestimating swelling, traveling without a companion, lodging far away or without an elevator, and comparing options without understanding what clinical follow-up includes. Ask about the scope of follow-up and contact channels. Edited social-media photos are not the average: ask for evolution examples at one, three, and six months when available, and accept your own timeline.

It also helps to define your main aesthetic goal clearly, share realistic reference photos, and ask what technical limitations apply in your specific case. That conversation reduces surprises and aligns expectations with what surgery can and cannot change.
